A gas water heater is a fairly simple machine, which is part of why it lasts. A burner under the tank heats the water, exhaust rises through a flue that runs up the center of the tank, and a gas control valve on the front acts as both thermostat and safety shutoff. That valve only lets gas reach the burner while it senses a flame, through a thermocouple on standing-pilot models or a flame sensor on electronic ignition models. Most failures trace back to one of those parts. In Temple, TX, we regularly find thermocouples that no longer hold the valve open, pilots starved by a lint-clogged flame arrestor screen, gas valves that have failed internally, and flue or vent problems that let exhaust spill back into the room.

Every gas water heater call starts with safety and then moves to testing. If you ever smell gas, leave the home first, call the gas utility from outside, and do not operate switches or try to relight anything. Once the area is safe, our Temple technicians work through the heater systematically: millivolt readings on the thermocouple, a close look at the pilot and main burner flames, gas pressure at the control valve, a draft test to make sure exhaust is not spilling back, and a CO check around the appliance. Scorch marks at the burner access, rust flakes on the burner, and a lazy yellow flame each point to a different problem, and we chase down the right one.

The fix is usually straightforward once the cause is known. A new thermocouple or flame sensor, a cleaned pilot tube and burner, a cleared flame arrestor screen, or a replacement gas control valve gets most heaters running again the same day. Vent problems, like a disconnected connector or a flue pipe without enough rise, are corrected on the spot. If sediment is rumbling in the tank, we flush it, and if the anode rod is spent, we can replace it. When replacement is the smarter move, we install new gas heaters sized to your household's first-hour demand, with proper atmospheric, power vent, or direct vent venting, a drip leg on the gas supply, and the expansion tank, drain pan, and strapping your local code calls for. How to Tell Something Is Wrong

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • Leaks at the drain valve, the fittings, or the bottom of the tank
  • Loud rumbling from the bottom of the tank
  • Soot or scorch marks around the burner access door
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • A vent pipe that has separated, rusted, or left scorch marks near the top of the heater
  • No hot water at all, with the burner never firing
  • A rotten-egg odor near the water heater, in which case get out and call the gas company first
  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Consider the risks first. Fuel piping, an open flame, and exhaust laced with carbon monoxide come with every gas water heater. A loose flare or threaded joint can leak gas, a vent that is disconnected or poorly sloped can spill exhaust back into the room, and a gas valve installed without a leak test is a risk you cannot see. Then there is the diagnostic side: a pilot that goes out can mean a weak thermocouple, a dirty pilot orifice, a clogged flame arrestor, a draft problem, or a failing gas valve, and replacing them one by one is slow and costly.

Professional service gets you a correct answer and a safe appliance. Instead of trial and error, we confirm thermocouple output, gas pressure, burner flame, and draft with proper tools. Every gas connection we make is tested for leaks before we leave, and vent connectors are secured with the right slope and clearances. When a replacement is needed, we size the new heater by first-hour rating, handle the permit where one is required, and add the expansion tank, sediment trap, drain pan, or seismic straps your home needs. Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Put these technician-approved steps to work at home:

  • Drain sediment from the bottom of the tank once or twice a year
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Keep the area around the heater clear, especially of gasoline, paint, and solvents
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years and replaced when it is worn
  • Set the gas valve near 120 degrees, because hotter water costs more and can scald
  • Check that the vent connector is secure and rising toward the chimney or flue
  • Make sure your home has CO alarms that work, especially with gas appliances
