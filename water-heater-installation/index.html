What makes a water heater installation different from a replacement? Usually, it is the new work. Adding a heater for an addition, relocating one out of a living space, upgrading to a larger tank or a tankless unit, or converting between gas, electric, and heat pump all require piping, venting, or wiring that did not exist before. In Temple, TX, that work also has to meet local code, which can mean permits, drain pans, expansion tanks, and seismic straps depending on the location. Temple Water Heater plans the whole job up front so the heater performs, the installation passes, and nothing gets improvised on the day.

Every installation starts with sizing and location. Tanks are sized by first-hour rating and tankless units by gallons per minute at your water's temperature rise, so the heater keeps pace with your busiest hour. Location is the next question. Gas units need a way to vent outdoors and enough air for combustion. Heat pump models need room, a space that stays fairly warm, and somewhere to drain condensate. An electric tankless unit may need a heavy dedicated circuit and enough panel capacity to support it. Above finished space or in an attic, the heater needs a drain pan piped somewhere safe. We work through all of it with you in plain language, then give you a firm price.

On installation day, nothing is left to guesswork. We run and pressure-test new gas piping, add a sediment trap, and route venting exactly to spec. For electric and heat pump units, the circuit is matched to the load. Water lines go in with accessible shutoffs and proper transitions between dissimilar metals, and the relief valve is piped full size to a safe discharge point. Code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps are added where required, and we handle the permit when your jurisdiction needs one. Last, the heater is filled, gas models are checked for draft and combustion, and the temperature is set safely. Planning a new installation? Call (254) 223-6745 for an assessment in Temple.

Why Professional Water Heater Installation Matters

Installing a new water heater means doing several trades' work at once. The gas piping must be sized for the heater plus every other appliance on the line, then pressure-tested until it is leak-free. Venting has to follow the manufacturer's rules for diameter, length, pitch, and clearance to combustibles, or carbon monoxide can spill back into the house. Electric and heat pump units need a circuit, breaker, and wire gauge matched to their load, and whole-house electric tankless units can draw more current than many panels have to spare. On the water side, there is more: shutoffs, dielectric transitions, a correctly rated relief valve with a full-size discharge line, and code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps that differ from place to place.

Professional service handles every trade involved, not just setting the heater. Sizing by first-hour rating or flow rate, tested gas piping, a vent routed to spec, a circuit matched to the load, and the expansion tank, pan, or straps your code requires are all included. Gas units get a draft and carbon monoxide check before we leave. The result is an installation that is safe, sized correctly, and ready for inspection. If a smaller or simpler heater fits your needs better, we will tell you directly.

Tips to Keep Your Water Heater Healthy

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Here's where to begin:

  • Descale tankless units once a year, or more often with hard water
  • Start a flushing routine early, before sediment has a chance to harden
  • Keep the temperature around 120 degrees; hotter settings raise the risk of scalding
  • Keep a clear path to the heater so it can be serviced and inspected easily
  • Nothing flammable belongs near a gas burner or its air supply
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years to protect the tank
  • Glance at the pan and discharge pipe every month or two; water there means it is time to call
