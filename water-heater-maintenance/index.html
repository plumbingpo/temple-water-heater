Here is what shortens most water heaters' lives: corrosion from the inside and mineral buildup on the bottom, both of which are largely preventable. As water heats, dissolved minerals come out of the water and settle into a layer of sediment on the tank floor. On a gas unit, that layer insulates the water from the burner, so the steel runs hotter and the tank starts to rumble and pop. On an electric unit, it buries the lower element until the element burns out. Meanwhile the anode rod, a metal rod designed to corrode so the tank does not, is slowly used up. Once it is gone, the tank itself becomes the target. In Temple, TX, we see this pattern every week, and most of it was preventable.

We work through a full checklist every time. The tank is drained and flushed to clear the sediment layer. The anode rod comes out for inspection and is swapped when little of it remains. The T&P relief valve is tested so we know it will open under pressure and close again cleanly. Gas heaters get a look at the burner, pilot assembly, thermocouple or flame sensor, flue, and draft. Electric heaters get element and thermostat testing and a check of the wiring connections. We also measure the air charge in the expansion tank, check the water pressure, set the thermostat to a scald-safe level unless a mixing valve allows higher storage, and inspect the drain valve, connections, and pan.

We maintain tankless units as well. In place of a sediment flush, they need periodic descaling, since hard water leaves mineral deposits in the heat exchanger that cut flow and efficiency. We isolate the unit, circulate a descaling solution through it, clean the inlet filter, check the vent and condensate line, and look at the error history for patterns. Whatever type you have, you receive a plain summary of the unit's condition and any parts approaching the end of their life. To schedule a visit in Temple, call (254) 223-6745.

Warning Signs to Watch For

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you money and keep a slow drip from turning into a flooded utility room. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Energy bills creeping up with no change in hot water use
  • More than a year since anyone flushed the tank
  • A tankless unit showing error codes or fluctuating temperatures
  • Hot water running out sooner than it used to
  • Brown or orange tint in the hot water only
  • A rotten-egg smell from the hot water
  • Moisture, drips, or rust around the base or fittings
  • A tank that pops and knocks during a heating cycle

The Benefits of Professional Water Heater Maintenance

The homeowner's side of maintenance is short: keep the area clear, watch for drips, and pay attention to new sounds. The rest is harder than online videos make it seem. Flushing involves scalding water and an old plastic drain valve that may crack or refuse to seal. Anode rods are often seized in place and need significant force, sometimes with a flexible rod for low-clearance spaces. An older T&P valve may not reseat once tested, which leaves you with a drip and a replacement to arrange. And on gas heaters, the burner and vent affect how combustion gases leave the house, which is not something to guess about.

We come prepared for what an older heater throws at us: replacement drain valves, anode rods sized for your tank, flexible anodes for tight clearances, T&P valves, and meters for testing elements and thermostats. For tankless systems, we bring the pump and descaling setup. Just as important, we know what to look for, from early corrosion at the fittings to a thermocouple starting to weaken, so you can fix small things before it becomes a failure. What you get is simple: a heater that lasts longer, runs more efficiently, and does not surprise you with a leak.

How to Prevent Water Heater Problems

Most of the failures we see could have been delayed for years with basic upkeep. Here are our recommendations:

  • Have the anode rod checked every few years and replaced when it is mostly gone
  • Stay on top of tankless descaling to avoid error codes and reduced flow
  • Ask about water treatment if flushing turns up heavy mineral buildup
  • Glance at the pan and connections regularly so small leaks are caught early
  • Keep at least a couple of feet clear around the heater for air and service access
  • Keep the thermostat near 120 degrees to limit scalding and slow sediment buildup
  • Schedule a flush every year, or every six months if you have hard water
